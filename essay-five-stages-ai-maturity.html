In brief

Awareness, literacy, and task-level adoption are not the same as transformation. The crucial shift is redesigning work, decisions, and accountability around what AI makes possible.

If you can name the problem, it is easier to tame and solve that problem. I learned this phrase during my MBA in 2019.

And right now it rings even louder in my head.

There is a specific conversation I keep having with leadership teams, and it goes roughly the same way every time. I ask how their AI transformation is progressing, and I get an enthusiastic answer about the licenses purchased across departments, employees using AI tools daily, measurable time savings on specific tasks, and a genuine sense of momentum.

Then I ask a follow-up question: has anything about how your organization is actually structured, how decisions get made, or how work flows between people and functions changed because AI exists?

Most of the time, the answer, after a pause, is no or not really.

This gap reflects a real, underappreciated structure of how organizations and individuals actually progress with AI, and most of the current confusion about why AI investments are underdelivering stems from collapsing five genuinely distinct stages into a single vague category called ‘doing AI.’

I want to walk through this carefully, because I think naming the stages precisely is one of the most useful things a leader can do right now, and because most organizations are stuck at a stage they believe they’ve already moved past.

The Five Stages of AI Maturity

  1. Stage 1: Awareness AI Awareness is usually the starting point, and it asks a simple question: What is AI? The outcome at this stage is knowledge, where people roughly understand what large language models are, what they can and cannot do, how they differ from previous generations of software, and why everyone is suddenly talking about them. This stage leads to conference attendance and a general vocabulary that lets people participate in conversations about AI without embarrassing themselves. However, on its own, worth almost nothing operationally, because knowing what AI is does not change a single thing about how anyone actually works.
  2. Stage 2: Literacy AI Literacy moves the question inward: how do I use AI? The outcome here is individual skill, where someone learns to write an effective prompt, understands the difference between asking a vague question and giving the tool real context, develops the habit of iterating on a first response instead of accepting it, and builds some working sense of when the tool is likely to be useful versus when it is likely to produce confident nonsense. At this stage, we can produce individual users genuinely capable of using AI. It does not, by itself, produce organizational change because literacy lives within individuals and disappears when they leave, are reassigned, or simply forget to apply it under deadline pressure.
  3. Stage 3: Adoption AI Adoption shifts the question toward application: where can AI help me? The outcome is better productivity on specific, identifiable tasks. This is the stage most organizations are currently in, and the one most organizations mistake for transformation. This stage produces visible, measurable results such as faster drafting, quicker summarization, or reduced time spent on specific repetitive tasks. It is genuine progress, where AI is layered onto existing workflows, roles, decision structures, and metrics, all of which remain otherwise unchanged. The organization is doing the same things it always did, just somewhat faster, with an AI tool inserted at specific points.
  4. Stage 4: Transformation AI Transformation is where the question becomes: how should work be redesigned because AI exists? The outcome requires new ways of working, requiring us to ask whether the current structure still makes sense at all, now that AI exists. It requires redesigning sequencing, handoffs, decision rights, and accountability structures around what AI actually enables, rather than preserving the existing structure and inserting AI wherever it can be squeezed in. This is genuinely difficult, disruptive work, and it is the stage at which most organizations that believe they are ‘doing AI transformation’ actually stall because they never leave adoption behind in the first place. It is possible to have excellent tool usage and an unchanged organization, and then call the combination transformation because the tools are impressive enough to create that impression.
  5. Stage 5: AI-Native The AI-Native Organization asks the most demanding question of all: if we were starting today, with AI available from day one, how would we design this organization? The outcome, when this question is genuinely pursued, is sustainable competitive advantage. It asks what would never have been built in the first place if AI had existed at the organization’s founding. Most current organizational structures (reporting lines, departmental boundaries, approval chains, role definitions) were designed under assumptions about information scarcity, analytical capacity, and coordination cost that AI has fundamentally changed. An organization designed from scratch today, with AI as a foundation rather than an add-on, would likely look structurally different in ways that go well beyond which tools employees use. Almost no existing organization has done this work, because it requires a willingness to dismantle structures that nobody currently has explicit permission to dismantle, even when everyone can sense those structures no longer quite fit.

Why the Third Stage Is Where Everyone Gets Stuck

The most important thing about this progression is not the five stages themselves, but the specific gap between Adoption and Transformation. This is where the overwhelming majority of organizations currently sit, and it is worth understanding exactly why the gap is so wide and so difficult to cross.

The thing about adoption is that it is comfortable because it adds to what already exists. Nobody’s role has to be eliminated, nobody’s authority has to shift, nobody has to have a difficult conversation about why a department that used to be essential is now smaller, or why a decision that used to require five layers of approval now requires one.

Adoption produces real, visible wins, the kind that show up nicely in a quarterly update, usually without requiring anyone in leadership to spend the political capital that structural change demands.

Transformation, on the other hand, is uncomfortable. It requires acknowledging that the organizational structure itself, not just the tools running through it, was built for a world that no longer fully exists. It requires redesigning decision rights, meaning some people gain authority while others lose it. It requires redesigning workflows, which means some roles become less central and others become more critical.

It requires leadership willing to spend real political capital on disruption whose benefits are diffuse and long-term. Most leadership teams, quite understandably, prefer the comfortable stage to the uncomfortable one, so they stay in the Adoption stage while describing it to themselves and their boards as Transformation.

I want to be fair to these leadership teams, because the discomfort is not irrational. Genuine transformation carries real risk, restructuring decision rights can go wrong, and redesigning workflows can disrupt operations that were working fine.

The Stage Almost Nobody Reaches

If the gap between Adoption and Transformation is wide, the gap between Transformation and AI-Native is even wider, and it is worth being honest about how few organizations will ever cross it, at least among those that already exist in something like their current form.

The AI-Native question ‘if we were starting today, how would we design this’ is not one that existing organizations can fully answer about themselves, because existing organizations are, by definition, not starting today. They carry history, sunk investment, existing relationships, existing talent structured around existing roles, and existing customers who expect existing patterns of interaction.

Genuinely redesigning from a blank slate is, for most established organizations, not actually available as an option, no matter how seriously the exercise is undertaken.

What is available, and what I think the most ambitious leadership teams should actually be doing, is using the AI-Native question as a diagnostic rather than a literal blueprint. You are not required to tear down the company and rebuild it, but honestly identify the specific gaps between what you would build today and what you currently have, and then decide, deliberately, which of those gaps are worth closing and in what order.

This is a more modest and achievable exercise than full reinvention, and it is also considerably more useful than the vague aspiration to ‘become AI-native’ that appears in so many current strategy documents without any real operational meaning.

The Practical Use of This Framework

The reason I laid out these five stages clearly is mostly diagnostic.

If you are a leader trying to understand where your own organization actually stands, the useful exercise is not to ask ‘Are we doing AI?’ Almost every organization will answer yes to that question, regardless of the stage they are in, because it is too vague to elicit an honest answer. The useful exercise is to ask, stage by stage, which question your organization is actually operating from right now.

  • Are people simply aware that AI exists and roughly what it does? That’s Awareness, not progress.
  • Do individuals across your organization have real, practiced skills in using AI tools well? That’s Literacy, and it matters, but it lives in individuals and will not transform anything on its own.
  • Is AI producing measurable productivity gains on specific tasks within your existing structure? That’s Adoption, and it is where almost everyone currently sits, often while believing they have already moved past it.
  • Has your organization actually redesigned how work flows, how decisions get made, and how accountability is structured, because AI now exists? That’s Transformation, and very few organizations can honestly claim it.
  • And finally, have you done the harder diagnostic work of asking what you would build today if you were starting from scratch, and used the gaps that exercise reveals to guide deliberate structural change? That’s the AI-Native question, and almost nobody has genuinely engaged with it yet.

Most leadership teams, if they answer these questions honestly, will find themselves solidly in Adoption, with pockets of genuine Literacy among their most engaged employees, and with Transformation existing mostly as a word in their strategy documents rather than as a description of anything that has actually happened.

Where This Leaves You

To separate yourself from your competitors over the coming years, the focus should shift from having the most AI licenses, the highest tool usage rates, or the most impressive individual employee skills. Those things matter, but they describe Literacy and Adoption, which many organizations have widely achieved, and no longer differentiate.

The advantage will come from organizations willing to do the harder, more uncomfortable work of Transformation, and from the smaller number willing to seriously engage with the AI-Native question as a diagnostic of what genuinely big structural change would require.

This is not a comfortable message, and I want to be honest about that rather than softening it.

Moving from Adoption to Transformation requires spending real political capital, disrupting structures that currently work well enough to make disruption feel unnecessary, and accepting real risk in exchange for benefits that will not be fully visible for some time.

Most leadership teams will choose not to do this and will continue describing their Adoption-stage progress in Transformation-stage language because that language is more flattering and requires nothing further of them.

The leaders who do choose to do this work will, within a few years, be operating with organizations that are structurally different from their competitors in ways that compound rather than merely accumulate.

That structural difference, not the tools themselves, is where the actual competitive advantage of this era will reside.

The organizations built around them are not, and the gap between them is going to become the story of the next decade, whether or not most leadership teams are currently ready to admit which side of that gap they are actually standing on.
