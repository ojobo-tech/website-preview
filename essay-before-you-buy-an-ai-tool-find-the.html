Should I buy an AI tool, learn how to prompt, or sign up for a course?

In my conversations with individuals, professionals, and business owners, this is the question I hear most often, and it usually comes with an underlying admission: they are not sure where to start. Most already have a few AI tabs open, a newsletter or two telling them what they are missing, and a growing feeling that they should have decided something by now.

If that sounds familiar, you are not alone. A new tool, a skill, and a course are all solutions, and solutions are easy to shop for before you know what you are trying to solve. Many people rush to purchase subscriptions, master prompt engineering, learn to build agents, or engage with automation vendors before identifying the problem worth solving.

In this article, I walk you through a different sequence: find the problem first, test AI on it cheaply, and only then decide what, if anything, to buy. It takes about a week, and all you need is your own work and a free account.

The real mistake is the order

You may buy or subscribe to a new AI tool with no use case attached, so it goes quiet after the first enthusiastic week. You take a course with nothing to apply what you learn to, so the lessons are theoretical. You may learn to write prompts with no task to practice on, so the skill never gets applied. But none of them answers the question of where AI actually helps you, so the question stays open while the months and the money go by.

The thesis fits in one line: start with the work, not the tool. Everything below is a way of doing exactly that.

AI is not just a chatbot

For many people, “AI” has come to mean a chat window. That is one kind of AI, and a useful one, but it is not the only kind and often not the one that pays back the most. Predicting which customers are likely to leave, forecasting demand, recommending products, routing requests, checking images for defects, and transcribing calls are all AI as well, and I believe the boring, practical applications are where much of the real return sits.

The problem should determine the AI category, and the category decides what you buy. The table in the section on matching problems to types of AI, further down, shows how the categories map to tools.

Step 1: Audit your own work

Think about a particular workflow you carry out and write it out end-to-end: what needs to happen at each step, what decisions need to be made, and who the steps depend on. Then spend thirty minutes marking the places in that write-up where time, accuracy, or quality is actually being lost. Four prompts help you spot candidates:

  • Work that repeats. Do you have to make the same report, reply, analysis, client follow-up, or code review again every day or week?
  • Work that involves drafting, summarizing, or sorting data or information. Emails, meeting notes, documents, requests that need to be categorized.
  • Decisions made with too little information. Places where you guess because the numbers or information are hard to pull together.
  • Bottlenecks where things wait on you. Approvals, answers, and handoffs that stall until you get to them.

Then track your tasks for a few days. For each one, note what it was, how long it took, and whether it felt tedious. A notes app or a simple spreadsheet is enough for this. The aim is a list of specific tasks, such as “reply to routine customer inquiries” or “compile the weekly sales numbers,” rather than broad areas like “marketing.”

Step 2: Run each candidate through four questions

Take the tasks that stood out and ask four questions of each. This is the Use Case Filter I use before anyone spends a dollar on AI.

  1. Is someone doing this manually today? If a person already does it, you have a baseline to compare against and a clear thing to improve. Who is doing this and how?
  2. Is the data already being captured? Drafting and summarizing only need the text in front of you. Prediction and recommendation may require historical data, such as past orders, past customers, and past outcomes. If that history is not recorded anywhere, your first project is to capture it, not to buy an AI tool.
  3. Is “80% accuracy” useful or dangerous? A tool that suggests email subject lines can miss often and still help. However, a tool that drafts a legal clause, a financial figure, sends automated client follow-ups, or writes a customer-facing claim cannot.
  4. What does the human do after the AI acts? Someone has to review, approve, or act on the output. If you cannot say who and how, the workflow is not yet ready for AI.

Narrow your list to the three strongest candidates. Any candidate who falls into the “high accuracy/risk” category (legal, financial, medical, or customer-facing) requires extra care, which is what Step 4 is for.

Step 3: Test one candidate with a free or low-cost tool

Pick one task and test it for real. For most ideas, a free or low-cost chat tool is the cheapest and fastest place to start, even if the problem ultimately gets solved by something else. The test tells you whether AI helps at all, and what kind of help you need.

The quality of the test depends on the context you give. Compare these two ways of asking for the same thing:

  • Lazy: “Summarize these customer emails.”
  • Briefed: “These are customer emails from the past week. Group them by topic, list the three most common complaints with a rough count for each, flag anything that looks urgent, and tell me where you are unsure.”

In the second prompt, you state the goal, the shape of the answer, and ask the tool to show its uncertainty. This is where the skill of prompting is put to the test in context: you find out how much skill matters for your task by trying it on your task.

Then compare the result with how you would normally do the work. Note three things: time saved, quality of the result, and the effort you spent fixing it. A tool that saves ten minutes and costs fifteen in corrections has not helped.

If the result is poor, the cause may not be the tool at all; it may be that you have not yet built the skill to use it well, and Step 5 covers what to do in that case.

Step 4: Verify before you trust

I have come to realize that “where AI helps me” and “where I can safely trust it” are different questions, and the test in Step 3 only answers the first. To get the best out of AI, there is still a responsibility to check the specific facts, numbers, names, and citations before they go anywhere that matters; sometimes AI output reads with the same confidence whether it is right or wrong.

For tools that predict rather than write: test the prediction on past data where you already know what happened, and see how often it would have been right. If you cannot verify the output cheaply, that signals how much weight it deserves, which is why a human must review the result every time.

Where AI is not worth the effort

Not every task deserves AI; here are some things to watch out for. If the task:

  • Happens rarely, so the setup time will never pay back.
  • Depends on a relationship, where the value is that a particular person is paying attention.
  • Carries high stakes and gives you no practical way to verify the result.
  • Is already quick, so learning and configuring a tool can end up costing more than it saves.
Finding that nothing in a task is worth automating is a valid result of this exercise. It means you kept your money and your attention on something that will actually move your work forward.

Step 5: Now decide what to buy

Your test has now answered the question you started with. Match what happened to what you do next:

  • The free tool did the job, but you hit its limits. Upgrade the tool; you now know what you would be paying for.
  • The tool was capable, but your results were poor. Build skill: practice giving richer context and iterating on the first answer. A course or a guided session is worth considering here, because you have a real task to apply it to.
  • The problem is prediction, recommendation, or inspection. Look at a specialized tool from the table above, or a data and analytics partner, rather than a prompting course.
  • You need this across a team or inside a complex workflow. That is the point for structured training or outside help, with your tested use case as the starting brief.
  • Nothing helped. Keep your money for now and run the exercise again after building some more skill or getting outside help.

The one-week exercise

Here is the whole process on one page. Save it or screenshot it. Pick your start date now and put the first task log in your calendar, because an exercise with no date attached tends to stay a good idea.

  1. Days 1 and 2: Log your tasks, including how long each one took and whether it felt tedious.
  2. Day 3: Flag your three strongest candidates and run each through the four questions.
  3. Days 4 and 5: Test one candidate with a free or low-cost tool, giving it real context.
  4. Day 6: Verify the output and score it on time saved, quality, and effort spent fixing it.
  5. Day 7: Decide what, if anything, to buy or learn, using Step 5.

Where this leaves you

The order that works is: problem, then test, then purchase. A deliberate week of this will tell you more about where AI helps you than months of sampling tools, and it leaves you with a decision you can explain to yourself, your team, or whoever approves the budget. If you are not sure where to start, you may also have more time than the pressure to act suggests.
